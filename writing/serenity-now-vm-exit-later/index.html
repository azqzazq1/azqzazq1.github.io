OFF-TOPIC / VMX / OFFICE POLITICS · OCT 2026

Serenity now. VM-exit later.

A harmless instruction, an unreasonable interception policy, and one very overworked hypervisor.

The guest has been running for four milliseconds without calling me. Four beautiful milliseconds. My calendar is clear. The tea is hot. I am beginning to believe we have reached an understanding.

Then the monitor flashes: VM EXIT.

Again? What is this, an emergency? Did someone unplug the virtual universe? No. The guest executed a perfectly ordinary instruction that happened to be on my interception list. I wrote the interception list. That is irrelevant. I feel ambushed.

A host with boundaries

In a traditional office, there's a front desk. Visitors sign in, the receptionist calls upstairs, and nobody interrupts the manager until there is a good reason. In a VMX-based virtual machine, the manager is the VMM. The guest is the office worker. A VM entry tells the worker to get on with it; a configured event can cause a VM exit, returning control to the manager.

I made a list of events worthy of my attention. The list was ambitious. Every interesting thing became worthy of attention. The guest is now contacting management constantly. We have accidentally built a call center.

“SERENITY NOW,” I whisper to the scheduler. “VM-EXIT LATER.” The processor is unmoved by the request.

The most expensive five minutes of my life

My first design: inspect every single event. Every register twitch! Every microscopic suspicion! Surely, if we can see everything, we can understand everything.

What I forgot is that observation has a cost. VM exits save and restore state, move control across a virtualization boundary, and invoke a handler. Some workloads tolerate this beautifully. Others turn my “harmless” observability policy into a slowdown large enough to qualify as an incident.

Now I am the guest's performance problem and the person investigating the guest's performance problem. I have an entire organizational chart. It is one man.

An incident report, naturally

  1. Guest executes work inside VMX non-root operation.
  2. A configured intercept causes a VM exit.
  3. VMM examines exit reason and relevant guest state.
  4. VMM records only the data needed for the hypothesis.
  5. VMM resumes the guest and measures the resulting overhead.
  6. I file a complaint against my own interception configuration.

This isn't an argument against instrumentation. It is an argument against pretending that more instrumentation is free. You need a research question before you decide where to stop the machine.

OWL0 learns to knock

For OWL0, the goal is not to throw a party below the kernel and invite every instruction. It is to test a specific observation boundary. Can a guest process monitor account for a request handled outside the guest's ordinary process model? What can a lower-layer observer record? What unavoidable footprints remain, such as CPU time and memory effects?

The experiment should answer those questions with a bounded protocol, targeted intercepts, independent timing data, and an honest list of what we have not proven. “The guest didn't list a process” does not mean “nothing could ever observe this.”

I explain this very calmly while trying to forcefully close seven debugger windows.

Tonight I will remove half my intercepts. I will ask the guest to call only when it is important. I will write down what “important” means. I will measure before boasting. This is growth.

Tomorrow, if the system is fast, I will take the credit. If it is slow, I will speak privately to the hypervisor about professional boundaries.

The VM exit was my configuration. I continue to believe the guest could have shown some consideration.
